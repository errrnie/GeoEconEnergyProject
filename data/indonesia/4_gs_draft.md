Indonesia is rich in coal and gas, and the data centres get the coal. Coal made two thirds of the country's electricity in 2025, above the government's own target, and power plants buy it at a capped US$70 a tonne. Indonesia exports about a third of its gas, mostly as LNG to China, Japan and Korea, and is now pulling some of it back home as local supply tightens.

Almost every data centre sits on Java. PLN says 99% of its data centre load is on the Java-Madura-Bali grid, which runs about 60% on coal. Batam is the exception. It lies 20 km from Singapore on its own 844 MW gas grid, and one 450 MW data centre contract equals more than half of that grid. Natuna gas that used to go to Singapore is now being piped into Batam. The island's water is the tighter limit: one reservoir supplies 80% of it, and residents blocked the road to Nongsa Digital Park in October 2026 after months of dry taps.

The economy grew 5.1% in 2025. The fastest-growing electricity demand is off PLN's grid, in captive coal plants at nickel smelters, which used 137 TWh in 2025. PLN expects data centres to need 25 GW by 2034, against about 580 MW today. The industry association raised its 2030 forecast from 2.7 GW to 11 GW within eight months.

Indonesia joined BRICS in January 2025, and Chinese firms control about three quarters of its nickel refining. Washington never singled it out on chips: there is no US licence requirement for AI chips, though Bloomberg reports that a Singapore firm under US scrutiny imported Nvidia hardware through an Indonesian unit. A US trade deal signed in February 2026 is not yet in force, and Indonesian goods now pay a 10% US duty.
