# Singapore notes

## Singapore's role: the hub that rations itself

Singapore is still Southeast Asia's largest data centre market. BMI puts live capacity at about 1.46 GW in 2026 (Technode, Jul 2026), up from about 1 GW and 70 sites when the moratorium was lifted in January 2022 (DCD) and about 1.4 GW in May 2024 (DCD). What Singapore sells is connectivity and trust. It has subsea cable landings, the region's interconnection hubs (Equinix SG1 to SG6, Digital Realty, STT GDC, Global Switch) and regional headquarters. AWS has run its first Asia Pacific region here for over a decade and pledged S$12bn more by 2028. Google has four data centres in Jurong West and US$5bn invested. Alibaba Cloud (2015), Huawei Cloud and TikTok's servers sit here too.

What Singapore lacks is spare power and land. Data centres already use about 7% of national electricity (MDDI, 2024), and almost all of that electricity comes from imported gas. So in 2019 the government paused new data centres, and since 2022 it has handed out capacity in small, competitive tranches: about 80 MW in the 2023 pilot call (Equinix, GDS, Microsoft, AirTrunk-ByteDance), at least 300 MW through efficiency under the May 2024 Green DC Roadmap, and 200 MW in August 2026 to Digital Realty, Equinix, Keppel and STT GDC, all on Jurong Island. Each tranche raised the bar, with PUE 1.25 at full load, Green Mark Platinum, and at least half the new capacity on green pathways such as biomethane, hydrogen, ammonia or fuel cells.

## Why capacity spilled to Johor and Batam

Hyperscale and AI campuses now come in 100 to 300 MW blocks. Singapore's whole 2026 allocation was 200 MW split four ways. The arithmetic pushed demand across the Causeway and the Singapore Strait. Princeton Digital made it explicit with its "SG+" strategy in 2023 (a 96 MW Batam campus first, then Johor). Nxera, DayOne and AirTrunk all now pair Singapore sites with Johor or Batam builds. DC Byte said in July 2025 that Johor had moved beyond its early role as a Singapore spillover market, with pipeline plans up to 5.7 GW. The January 2025 Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone does not name data centres, but it covers Sedenak, Johor's main data centre cluster, lists digital economy and energy among its 11 sectors, and commits both sides to renewable power trade. In practice Singapore keeps the latency-sensitive network core and the head offices; Johor and Batam supply megawatts.

## Scarcity as policy

Rationing lets the state pick winners and attach conditions. The 2026 winners were US and Singapore landlords; no Chinese operator won in the second round. Scarcity also raises the value of existing Singapore capacity and pushes operators toward on-site fuel cells, liquid cooling and Jurong Island's hydrogen and ammonia ecosystem. The 20 ha Jurong Island park (up to 700 MW of power) is the government's long-run answer, but BMI expects it to ease shortages only slowly.

## Takeaways

1. Singapore grows by permission. New capacity arrives in 50 to 300 MW government tranches tied to efficiency and green fuel targets. Market demand alone does not unlock power.
2. The power problem is a gas problem. Gas made 93.1% of electricity in 2025 (EMA). Indonesian pipeline contracts run out by 2028 and Qatar's LNG stopped in April 2026, so data centre load is exposed to world LNG prices. The 6 GW import target for 2035 is the exit route, and most projects are still conditional.
3. The region is one market with three addresses. Singapore holds the network and contracts, Johor and Batam hold the power-hungry halls. JS-SEZ and cross-border power trade formalise that split.
4. Neutral hub status is under strain. Nvidia billed 18% of FY2025 revenue to Singapore while under 2% shipped there; Singapore charged three men in a US$390m server fraud case in February 2025; and Singapore-registered Megaspeed faces US and Singapore probes over alleged chip diversion (allegations, denied). US BIS guidance in May 2026 now requires licences for chips going to China-parented firms anywhere.
5. Chinese platforms adapt by re-domiciling. GDS's international arm became Singapore-headquartered DayOne, raised money from US, Chinese and Indonesian investors, and filed for a US IPO in October 2026.

## Draft: the 4 Gs

**Geology.** Singapore has no oil, no gas, no rivers for hydro and little land for solar. Yet gas made 93% of its electricity in 2025, all of it imported. About half arrives by pipe from Indonesia and Malaysia and half as LNG. The Indonesian pipe contracts run out by 2028, and the Qatari cargoes stopped in April 2026 when war hit the Gulf.

**Geography.** The island is small and full. Its strength is position. Subsea cables land here, and Johor and Batam sit within 20 km. That is why Singapore keeps the network hubs while the large server halls go next door.

**Growth.** Data centres use about 7% of the country's power. Peak demand was 8.2 GW in 2025 and could reach 12.5 GW by 2034 if data centres keep growing. The government rations new capacity, 80 MW in 2023 and 200 MW in 2026, and plans a 700 MW green park on Jurong Island, the petrochemical island.

**Geopolitics.** Singapore wants to be the place everyone trusts. American, Chinese and local operators all run sites here. That trust is now tested. Nvidia bills nearly a fifth of its revenue through Singapore, a 2025 fraud case involved servers that may have held Nvidia chips, and US officials are investigating a Singapore-registered firm accused of moving chips to China, which it denies.
