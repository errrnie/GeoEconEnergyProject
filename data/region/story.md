# How compute came to Southeast Asia and why it went where it did

## 1. The Singapore decade (2010 to 2018)

Southeast Asia's cloud story starts in one city. AWS opened its first Asia Pacific region in Singapore in April 2010 [1], and Google followed with its first Southeast Asian region there in 2017 [2]. It was the region's connectivity and business hub, and for most of the decade, "Southeast Asia" on a cloud map meant Singapore, with a few Chinese clouds such as Alibaba in Kuala Lumpur from 2017 [3].

## 2. The moratorium and the spillover (2019 to 2022)

In 2019 Singapore stopped approving new data centres because they used too much land, water and energy. It lifted the pause in January 2022 but kept new capacity on a ration [4]. Demand did not wait. It went across the causeway to Johor, which had land and sat next door to Singapore. AMRO's review of Malaysia says Johor's rise came from its closeness to Singapore and was helped by the ban [5]. Batam, Indonesia's island just south of Singapore, opened Nongsa Digital Park with Singapore's backing in 2018 [6], but it stayed small (about 8MW live in 2025) [7].

Meanwhile US clouds began building in-country regions where data rules and market size justified it: Jakarta got Google in 2020 [8] and AWS in 2021 [9].

## 3. The AI boom meets US-China decoupling (2022 to 2024)

Two things then happened at once. AI made data centres bigger and hungrier for power, and US export controls from October 2022 split the chip world. Southeast Asia became a place both camps could build. ISEAS calls Malaysia a "dual-stack" host with almost every major US and Chinese cloud, and notes a Chinese footprint hidden inside colocation sites [10].

Malaysia moved fastest on the thing AI needs most. Its utility TNB launched a Green Lane in 2023 that aimed to cut high-voltage grid connection from 36 to 48 months to about 12 [11]. The mega pledges followed: Microsoft US$1.7bn in Indonesia [12] and US$2.2bn in Malaysia, AWS regions in Malaysia (2024) and Thailand (2025), and Google commitments in both [13].

## 4. Constraint and rules (2025 to 2026)

The boom ran into limits. The US AI Diffusion Rule of January 2025 put Singapore and the rest of the region in a capped Tier 2 [14]; it was rescinded in May 2025 [15], and diversion enforcement took its place. Hosts then started braking themselves. Johor stopped approving water-heavy Tier 1 and 2 data centres in November 2025 [16]. Thailand paused 49 projects under construction in September 2026, more than the 35 it had running [17]. Singapore kept rationing, awarding 200MW in its 2026 call [18]. Money still flowed: Google added US$15bn for Malaysia in November 2026 [19].

## Why Johor won the first round

Johor had four things at once. It sat next to the region's cable and cloud hub, it had land, TNB promised grid connections in about a year [11], and the state approved projects quickly [10]. By mid-2026 Johor had 1,110MW operating, more than Singapore's 1,068MW, and Malaysia had 1,039MW under construction, the most in Asia Pacific [20]. No other market combined proximity, power and speed.

## Who is next

**Indonesia** has the strongest case. Greater Jakarta had 356MW operating and a 1,699MW pipeline in H1 2026 [21], all three US clouds are live, and Batam just signed a 450MW power deal [22]. Its weakness is a grid about 82% fossil fuel [23].

**Vietnam** has opened the door legally. Foreign firms can own 100% of a data centre [24], and a data law since July 2025 pushes data onshore [25]. But it has only about 73MW operating and faces 2 to 3 year waits for 100MW connections [26].

**The Philippines** has the biggest ambition and the smallest base: about 140MW live, with power the main constraint [27]. Its 2026 masterplan targets 1.5GW by 2033 [28], and AWS is discussing a region [29].

## Which deep dive to add next

Indonesia. It is the largest market after the three already covered, it has the full set of US and Chinese clouds, and its pipeline is about five times its operating base [21]. It also extends the Johor story through Batam and tests the coal question that Malaysia and Thailand only partly face. Vietnam and the Philippines are still more plans than buildings, so they fit better as short sections inside a regional page.

## Five takeaways

1. Singapore built the hub, and its 2019 pause sent the overflow to Johor.
2. Speed to power, more than tax breaks, decided where AI capacity landed.
3. US-China decoupling made Southeast Asia a neutral host for both stacks.
4. Since late 2025 the leading hosts are rationing water, power and approvals.
5. Indonesia is the next big test; Vietnam and the Philippines are still mostly promises.
