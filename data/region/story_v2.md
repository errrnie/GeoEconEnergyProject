## 1. The Singapore decade (2010 to 2018)

Southeast Asia's cloud story starts in Singapore. AWS opened its first Asia Pacific region there in April 2010 [1]. Alibaba Cloud opened a data centre there in 2015 and made the city its overseas headquarters [2], and Google opened its first Southeast Asian region there in 2017 [3]. The city also had the region's densest cluster of subsea cable landings [4]. Chinese firms moved early into Malaysia too. Huawei invested there in 2017 and Alibaba opened a Kuala Lumpur data centre that year, seven years before the first US cloud region [5].

## 2. The pause and the land grab (2019 to 2022)

In 2019 Singapore stopped approving new data centres over their carbon footprint and heavy use of land, water and energy. It lifted the pause in January 2022 and has rationed new capacity since [6]. Developers looked across the causeway to Johor, close enough to Singapore's cables to keep latency low. AMRO says Singapore's ban helped boost investment there [4]. GDS, now DayOne, announced its first 54MW in Johor in 2021, and ByteDance moved into a Bridge Data Centres facility in 2022 [5]. Building took longer. Johor's capacity was almost zero in 2020 and about 400MW in 2024 [4]. Batam opened Nongsa Digital Park with Singapore's backing in 2018 [7] but had only about 8MW live in 2025 [8].

US clouds also went to the region's biggest market. Indonesia loosened its data localisation rule for private firms in 2019 while keeping it for public bodies [9]. Google opened a Jakarta region in 2020 and AWS followed in 2021 [10][11].

## 3. AI demand and the build-out (2022 to 2024)

ChatGPT set off a spending race among the hyperscalers [5]. In October 2022 US export controls split the chip world [12]. Johor offered cheap land, fast state approvals and national incentives such as DESAC, and Malaysia had the lowest average power tariff among the five largest ASEAN economies [4][5]. In August 2023 the utility TNB launched a Green Lane that aimed to cut high-voltage grid connection from 36 to 48 months to about 12 [13].

Microsoft committed US$1.7bn to Indonesia and US$2.2bn to Malaysia in 2024 [14][15]. AWS opened regions in Malaysia in 2024 and Thailand in 2025 [16][17], and Google committed to both countries [18][19]. ISEAS calls Malaysia a "dual-stack" host with almost every major US and Chinese cloud, and argues that colocation sites hide a larger Chinese footprint [5].

## 4. Constraint and rules (2025 to 2026)

Johor's operating capacity more than doubled in 2025, from 401MW to 897MW [20]. Export controls now pulled Chinese demand offshore too. After the US curbed sales of Nvidia's H20 in April 2025, Alibaba and ByteDance trained more of their models in Southeast Asian data centres, within the rules [21].

The US AI Diffusion Rule of January 2025 put the whole region in a capped Tier 2 [22]. It was rescinded in May 2025, and enforcement against chip diversion took its place [23]. Malaysia began requiring permits to export US AI chips in July 2025 [24].

Then hosts braked. Malaysia revised electricity tariffs in July 2025, which AMRO expects to raise data centre bills by 10 to 14% [4]. Johor stopped approving water-heavy Tier 1 and 2 data centres in November 2025 [25]. Thailand paused 49 projects under construction in September 2026, more than the 35 it had running [26]. Singapore kept rationing, awarding 200MW in 2026 to operators who each committed to over half green power [27]. Google added US$15bn for Malaysia in November 2026 [28].

## Where things stand

By mid-2026 Johor had 1,110MW operating, about level with Singapore, which sources put at 1,068MW to 1,118MW [29][30]. Malaysia had 1,039MW under construction, the most in Southeast Asia, and Southeast Asia held about half of everything under construction in Asia Pacific [20]. The [Singapore](singapore/index.html) and [Malaysia](malaysia/index.html) deep dives cover the two leaders.

## Beyond Johor and Singapore

**Thailand** drew AWS and Google cloud regions and power requests far beyond its grid plan before its September 2026 pause [26]. The [Thailand deep dive](thailand/index.html) follows why it braked.

**Indonesia** has the strongest case for growth. Greater Jakarta had 356MW operating and a 1,699MW pipeline in H1 2026 [20][29], and all three big US clouds now run regions there [32]. DayOne's 450MW Batam power deal could make Batam a second Johor [31]. About 82% of Indonesia's power comes from fossil fuels [30]. See the [Indonesia deep dive](indonesia/index.html).

**Vietnam** has let foreign firms fully own data centres since January 2025 [33]. It has about 73MW operating and reported waits of 2 to 3 years for large grid connections [35]. See the [Vietnam deep dive](vietnam/index.html).

**The Philippines** has about 140MW live, with power the main constraint [36], and a plan for 1.5GW by 2033 [37].

## Five takeaways

1. Singapore's 2019 pause sent developers to Johor, and AI demand after 2022 filled it.
2. Fast grid connections, cheap land and power, and nearby subsea cables decided where capacity landed.
3. Chinese clouds came before the chip war. Export controls later added Chinese AI training as a new source of demand.
4. Hosting both US and Chinese stacks now means policing US chip rules.
5. Since late 2025 the leading hosts ration water, power and approvals, yet pledges keep coming.
