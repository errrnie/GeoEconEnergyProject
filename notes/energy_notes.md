# Energy notes: does Malaysia's data centre bet become an energy import bet?

Researched 11 Nov 2026. Every number below has a row in `energy.csv` with its source URL and a confidence rating. Figures marked "student estimate" or "derived" are my own arithmetic, not official numbers.

## Short answer

Yes, mostly. Peninsular Malaysia already burns imported coal for more than half its power. It is now replacing that coal with gas, and the extra gas will come more and more from imported LNG as domestic fields decline. Data centres are the main new source of demand, so they speed up the LNG shift. On top of that, the government cut a gas-fired capacity target date from 2032 to 2030.

## 1. Generation mix

- **Peninsular net generation:** 149.3 TWh in 2024, up from 141.1 TWh in 2023 (Energy Commission, ST).
- **National generation:** about 211 TWh in 2024 and 210 TWh in 2025 (Energy Institute via YCharts, medium confidence).
- **Peninsula mix (Grid System Operator data via Reuters):**
  - 2024: coal 56.5%, gas 36.4%, solar 2.1%.
  - 2025: coal 58.6%, gas 33.1%, solar 2.4%. Coal's share rose in 2025.
- **Swing to gas in 2026:**
  - April 2026 gas share was 42.6%, a record 5.54 TWh, against coal at 51.2%.
  - Demand that month was up 11.5% year on year.
- **National mix (Ember):** coal 43%, gas 37%, hydro about 17%. Sarawak hydro dilutes the coal share.
- **Fuel burned for Peninsular power in 2024:** 900,941 TJ of coal and 424,258 TJ of gas (ST, National Energy Balance basis).
- **Implied gas fleet heat rate (derived):** about 7.8 GJ/MWh.

## 2. Capacity, peak and reserve margin (Peninsula)

- **Installed grid-connected capacity:** 28,192 MW in 2025.
- **Capacity mix in 2025:** coal 43.2%, gas 40.2%, hydro 8.2%, solar 6.2% (ST booklet).
- **Peak demand records:**
  - 21,049 MW on 28 May 2025 (ST booklet).
  - 21,583 MW on 8 Jun 2026 (The Edge, from GSO data).
  - 22,014 MW on 6 Aug 2026 (Utusan). Only one source reports this figure, so confidence is medium.
- **Peak demand projections:** 21.3 GW in 2026, rising to 33.5 GW in 2035, a 5.1% compound annual growth rate (JPPPET via PETRA).
- **Old forecasts already overtaken:** a March 2024 forecast put 2030 demand at 20,049 MW, and actual demand has already passed it.
- **Reserve margin:**
  - Official: 25% in 2025, down from 29%. PETRA says it will stay at 25-27% through 2035.
  - The Edge's calculation: about 23.4% headline, but only about 13% once unavailable plant is excluded.
  - Malaysia imported 300 MW from Thailand in Sep-Nov 2025 and again in June 2026.

## 3. Planned capacity

- **The "11.8 GW of new gas by 2033" figure could not be verified.** No source I found uses it. The verified figures are:
  - **ST consultation paper (May 2026):** eight indicative IPP gas plants totalling 8,348 MW, coming online 2028-2031 and needing 1,587 mmscfd of gas. All are subject to final investment decision.
  - **Economy Minister:** 9 GW of extra gas capacity. The target was 2032 in Sep 2026 and was moved to 2030 on 2 Nov 2026. He said it is driven "mainly" by demand growth, not coal replacement. He also said power-sector gas demand roughly doubles by 2032.
  - **NEWGEN26 tender:** up to 7,848 MW of new gas for 2029-2031, against up to 6,930 MW of possible retirements in the same window (The Edge).
  - **Wood Mackenzie:** Malaysia needs 9.4 GW of new gas by 2030, but only 5.9 GW is likely to be delivered. Turbine lead times are up to 5 years.
  - **Moody's:** 14-17 GW of new generation needed by 2035, costing RM80-95 billion over 10 years.
- **Coal retirements:**
  - Kapar, 1,475 MW, July 2029.
  - Janamanjung, 2,070 MW, August 2030.
  - Tanjung Bin, 2,100 MW, September 2031.
  - About 12.1 GW of coal retires from 2029 in total (HLIB). Full phase-out is set for 2044.
- **Solar and storage:** the LSS6 tender covers 2.5 GW of solar plus 1.25 GW of batteries, due online by end-2029. Installed solar nationally was about 5.7 GW in 2025.
- **Is data centre demand driving new gas?**
  - The Economy Minister, ST and TNB all link new gas capacity to data centre and industrial demand.
  - ST's CEO said no new gas capacity arrives in 2027, so the existing fleet must be "optimised" until then.

## 4. Fuel supply security

- **Coal is 100% exposure to imports on the Peninsula:**
  - 2025 thermal coal imports were a record 36.3 Mt.
  - By origin: Indonesia 74.6%, Australia 15.1%, Russia 7.0% (Russian volumes up 29%).
  - Indonesia plans to cut output to 600 Mt in 2026, from 790 Mt. Malaysia held supply talks with Jakarta in Feb 2026.
- **Gas sources for the Peninsula:**
  - East coast offshore fields, processed at Kerteh and piped through the Peninsular Gas Utilisation network.
  - The Thailand-Malaysia pipeline. Petronas raised volumes on it to 400 mmscfd in Apr 2025 after the Putra Heights fire.
  - LNG through RGT-1 at Sungai Udang, Melaka (500 mmscfd, about 3.8 Mtpa).
  - LNG through RGT-2 at Pengerang (735 mmscfd by 2028; about 3.5 Mtpa today per Argus).
  - Domestic LNG shipped from Sarawak and offshore fields: 446 kt in 2026 to late May, versus 150 kt in all of 2025.
- **Regas terminals in the pipeline:** RGT-3 at Lumut (500 mmscfd, 2029) and RGT-Yan in Kedah.
- **LNG import volumes:** 2.1 Mt in 2021, 3.3 Mt in 2024 and 2.26 Mt in 2025. The 2025 dip came because coal ran harder.
- **Imported LNG's role in power:** it was about 32% of gas used for power in Jan-May 2025 (Argus).
- **Long-term contracts from 2028:**
  - Qatar: 2 Mtpa for 20 years.
  - Woodside: 1 Mtpa (medium confidence).
  - US projects: two contracts of 1 Mtpa each (medium confidence).
- **Domestic decline:**
  - Production fell from 79.3 bcm in 2019 to 73.2 bcm in 2020.
  - The IEA reported a further 3% drop in Jan-May 2025.
  - Wood Mackenzie says Peninsular offshore fields are in long-term decline.
  - Petronas said in June 2025 that Malaysia would need significant LNG imports within 4-5 years.
- **Price exposure:**
  - The Gas Framework Agreement caps regulated power gas at 800 mmscfd and expires on 31 Dec 2027. Gas above the cap is priced against LNG (Tier 2). In H1 2025, Tier 2 was RM44.76/MMBtu against RM34.14 for Tier 1.
  - LNG-fired power costs about 29% more than power from local gas (Reuters).
  - After Hormuz closed in March 2026, Asian LNG rose from about USD10.6 to 15.8/MMBtu. Qatar says full repair of Ras Laffan could take up to five years, and Qatar is Malaysia's biggest new LNG contract.

## 5. Tariffs and who pays

- **RP4 (from 1 Jul 2025):**
  - The average base tariff is 45.40 sen/kWh, up from 39.95 in RP3.
  - Allowed capex is RM42.8 billion for 2025-2027, including RM16.3 billion of contingent capex.
- **Automatic Fuel Adjustment (AFA):**
  - It passes fuel costs through monthly.
  - It was +3.59 sen/kWh in July 2026, and TNB forecast about +8-9 sen/kWh for Aug-Oct 2026.
  - About 85% of households (600 kWh/month or less) are exempt.
- **Data centres:**
  - Since 1 Jul 2025 they are billed under a new Ultra High Voltage tariff category. No published rate was found.
  - The Connected Load Charge was raised to stop operators reserving capacity they do not use.
  - They must source at least 30% renewable energy.
  - The government states a "cost causation" principle, so households should not bear data centre grid costs. It has published no bill-impact numbers.
- **CRESS:** the system access charge for firm supply was cut from 25 to 20 and then 14 sen/kWh. Registered projects total about 3.15 GW.

## 6. NETR and cross-border trade

- **NETR targets:**
  - Renewables reach 40% of capacity by 2035 and 70% by 2050. The level was about 30% in 2025.
  - Coal capacity halves by 2035 and ends by 2044.
- **Why data centres complicate this:** the targets are in capacity terms. Data centres need 24/7 power, and their 30% renewable rule leaves about 70% of their energy coming from a grid that is mostly coal and gas.
- **Cross-border trade is small next to data centre demand:**
  - LTMS-PIP: 200 MW.
  - ENEGEM: up to 300 MW to Singapore, after 50 MW in the first auction.
  - The interconnector to Singapore is about 1,300 MW (low confidence).
- **Direction of flows:** Malaysia exports green power to Singapore while importing 300 MW from Thailand at peaks.

## 7. Scenario inputs

- **Data centre draw over time:**
  - 603 MW in Jun 2025 (47% of 1,276 MW declared).
  - 1,054 MW in Mar 2026.
  - About 1.26 GW in Jun 2026.
  - 1,374 MW in Aug 2026, which is 58.6% of 2,346 MW contracted.
  - Growth peaked at a record of about 200 MW per quarter in Q1 2026.
- **Load factor:** the government's 2030 projection is 39,289 GWh on 8,391 MW, which implies a 53% load factor on maximum demand (derived). The 2026 figure of 10,544 GWh is an average of 1,204 MW, about 88% of the August draw. This suggests running sites run flat, but much contracted capacity sits idle.
- **PUE:** design targets are 1.4 for hyperscale and 1.6-1.7 for colocation (MITI/MIDA guidelines). PUE is already included in metered grid draw, so I do not apply it again.
- **Heat rate:**
  - Central: 7.8 GJ/MWh, the Peninsular gas fleet in 2024 (derived from ST data).
  - Efficient: 7.34 GJ/MWh, the newest US CCGTs at 6,960 Btu/kWh HHV (EIA).
- **Conversions:** 1 Mt LNG = 1.36 bcm; 1 bcm = 40 PJ; so 1 Mt LNG = 54.4 PJ (Snam, citing IEA). With 52 GJ/t instead, the LNG tonnages below rise by about 4.6%.

### Three illustrative 2030 scenarios (student estimates)

**Method.** I assume all extra data centre demand above the 2026 level (10.544 TWh) is met at the margin by gas. This ignores grid losses, solar and coal, so it is an upper bound on gas per TWh.

- **Gas energy (PJ)** = extra TWh x heat rate (GJ/MWh), because 1 GJ/MWh equals 1 PJ/TWh.
- **bcm** = PJ / 40.
- **Mt LNG** = bcm / 1.36.

**The cases.**

- **Low:** 3.0 GW average load. The Aug 2025 to Aug 2026 trend adds roughly 0.7-0.8 GW of draw a year, which gives about 4 GW by 2030. At about 75% utilisation that is roughly 3 GW average.
- **Mid:** the government's own 2030 projection, 39,289 GWh. Your suggested "contracted capacity fully used" (2,346 MW x 8,760 h = 20.6 TWh) falls below the Low case, so I show it only as a reference row.
- **High:** all 8.3 GW committed by TNB, at an 85% load factor. 85% is a rounded assumption, close to the 88% implied for running sites in 2026.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low: 3.0 GW x 8,760 h | 26.3 | 12.4% | 17.6% | 15.7 | 123 / 3.07 / 2.26 | 2.89 / 2.12 |

**Worked example (High).**

1. 8.3 GW x 0.85 x 8,760 h = 61.8 TWh.
2. 61.8 - 10.5 = 51.3 TWh extra.
3. 51.3 TWh x 7.8 PJ/TWh = 400 PJ.
4. 400 / 40 = 10.0 bcm.
5. 10.0 / 1.36 = 7.35 Mt LNG.

**Comparison with imports and import capacity.**

- **Against actual LNG imports:** Malaysia imported 2.26 Mt in 2025 and 3.3 Mt in 2024. The Low case alone adds about one year of 2025 imports. The Mid case adds about 1.2-1.8 times recent imports. The High case adds 2.2-3.3 times.
- **Against regas capacity:** existing Peninsular capacity is about 7.3 Mtpa on Argus nameplate figures, or about 9.4 Mt/yr if ST's 1,235 mmscfd is converted. RGT-3 brings the total to about 13.2 Mt/yr. The High case would use most of today's regas capacity on its own.
- **Against new contracts:** the Qatar, Woodside and US deals total about 5 Mtpa from 2028, roughly the Mid case.
- **Total, not just incremental, data centre gas in 2030** at the central heat rate: Low 3.8, Mid 5.6, High 8.9 Mt LNG equivalent.

**Caveats.**

- Not all of this gas would be imported. Domestic supply, Thailand-Malaysia pipeline gas and Sarawak LNG cover part of it.
- Some data centre demand will be met by coal until 2029-2031 and by CRESS solar. The 30% renewable rule could cut the gas need by up to 30% in energy terms, less after firming.
- The heat rate basis (GCV or NCV) in the ST data is not stated, so treat results as plus or minus 10%.

## Conflicts between sources

- **Gas capacity target year:** the Star (9 Sep 2026) headline says "9 GW by 2044", Reuters says 2032, and SunBiz (2 Nov 2026) says it was moved to 2030. 2044 is the coal phase-out year, so the Star headline likely mixed the two up.
- **Data centre commitments:** 8.3 GW across 59 projects (RHB) versus 8.35 GW across 61 projects (Kenanga, June 2026). Separately, the DPM gives 12.3 GW of "approved supply capacity" (Aug 2026) and PETRA gives 6,041 MW supplied to 45 operating data centres. These measure different stages (approved, contracted, energised, drawn), so the page should not add them up.
- **Gas share of capacity:** the ST booklet says 40% in 2025, while the ST CEO said "about 55%" in Sep 2026. The 55% may include extended PPAs, or may be national or rounded.
- **Peninsular energy totals:** ST's GEF document gives 149,301 GWh net generation for 2024. The ST booklet gives 141,738 GWh (2024) and 143,973 GWh (2025), probably on a different definition. For the 2025 mix bar I scaled the 2024 GEF figure by the booklet's growth, about 151.7 TWh (derived).
- **National total:** Ember's 2023 hydro figure of 32 TWh at 17% implies about 188 TWh. The Energy Institute gives 202.8 TWh for 2023. These are different coverage choices, so I kept each with its own source in `energy_mix.csv`.
- **Reserve margin:** officially 25% (2025) and 25-27% through 2035, but The Edge's adjusted 13% and Moody's narrowing headroom tell a tighter story.
- **Power-sector gas volumes:** about 856 mmscfd (ST booklet, label inferred) and "about 700 now" (TNB CEO, Oct 2025). The 424,258 TJ in 2024 converts to roughly 1,000-1,100 mmscfd. The volume figures may cover GFA gas only.
- **RGT-2 capacity:** 3.5 Mtpa (Argus) versus 735 mmscfd (ST, about 5.6 Mtpa, "expected capacity in 2028"), which suggests an expansion.
- **LSS6 size:** 2.5 GW versus 2.65 GW depending on outlet.

## Energy security takeaways (for the page)

1. Peninsular Malaysia already depends on imported fuel for most of its power: in 2025, imported coal made 58.6% of its electricity, and three quarters of that coal came from Indonesia.
2. The planned exit from coal runs through gas, and with domestic fields declining and new LNG contracts starting in 2028, extra data centre demand will largely be met with imported LNG.
3. If data centres use the government's own 2030 projection of 39 TWh, the extra gas equals roughly 4 Mt of LNG a year, more than Malaysia imported in 2024 or 2025.
4. The grid has less slack than the headline 25% reserve margin suggests: peak demand passed 22 GW in August 2026, and no new gas plant arrives before 2028-2029.
5. The main new LNG supplier, Qatar, sits behind the Strait of Hormuz, whose 2026 closure pushed Asian LNG prices up by about half; about 85% of households are exempt from the monthly fuel surcharge, but industry and data centres are not.
