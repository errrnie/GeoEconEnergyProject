Malaysia's Compute Bet

The US and China build most of the world's AI infrastructure at home. A handful of middle powers now host the overflow, and Malaysia has one of the largest data centre pipelines outside the US and China. This page follows how that happened, whose computing it serves, and what it asks of Malaysia's power and water.

6 · Chip access and security

Malaysia was put in the middle tier, and the US is still watching

In January 2025 the US sorted the world into three tiers for advanced AI chips. Close allies could buy freely, embargoed countries like China could not buy at all, and everyone in between, Malaysia included, faced a national cap. The rule was scrapped four months later, but the worry behind it, chips leaking to China through third countries, did not go away.

Malaysia's position today

As far as public records show, no US licence or cap applies to Malaysia as a country, although Commerce said in May 2025 it would add requirements for Malaysia and Thailand and has not published them. Instead Malaysia polices itself. Since 14 July 2025 anyone moving US AI chips out of or through Malaysia needs a Strategic Trade Permit, and the October 2025 US trade deal asked Malaysia to cooperate on US export controls, though that deal remains unratified and under review after the US Supreme Court struck down the tariffs behind it. The pressure now runs through ownership. Supplying top chips to a Chinese-parented tenant in Johor requires a US licence, while supplying a US hyperscaler next door does not.

The Megaspeed case

Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud firm that began as the overseas arm of Chinese game company 7Road, ran a data centre in Sedenak, Johor, through a Malaysian unit, and its largest site was in Malaysia. Bloomberg counted at least US$4.6 billion and about 136,000 Nvidia GPUs imported by its Malaysian and Indonesian units from 2023 to November 2025, including about US$787 million of GB300s brought into Malaysia that November, after Nvidia says it stopped selling to the firm. US prosecutors are reportedly investigating. Nobody has been charged. Megaspeed denies any diversion and says on-site reviews by the US Commerce Department, Malaysia's trade ministry and Nvidia found no violations; Nvidia says it has no reason to believe its secrets were compromised. In February 2026 Bain's Bridge Data Centres, its Malaysian landlord, gave the 68.4 MW meant for Megaspeed to another tenant.

Why this is an energy security question too

Proven diversion, like the two US prosecutions involving Malaysia, invites tighter rules on the whole country, and tighter rules can leave powered buildings in Johor waiting for chips. TNB's five-year take-or-pay contracts protect the utility, but the land, water and new gas plants are committed either way. When the chips do arrive, they mostly serve foreign-owned clouds, while the electricity, water and grid risk stay in Malaysia.

7 · Energy security

The data centre bet is turning into a fuel import bet

Peninsular Malaysia, where almost all the data centres sit, already makes most of its power from imported coal. It plans to retire that coal and build gas instead, just as its own gas fields decline. So more and more of each new megawatt of data centre load will run on imported LNG.

Who carries the price risk

Malaysia's biggest new LNG contract, 2 million tonnes a year from Qatar from 2028, sits behind the Strait of Hormuz. When the strait was disrupted in March 2026, Asian LNG prices rose by about half. Fuel costs pass through a monthly surcharge, but about 85% of households are exempt, so heavier users, industry and data centres absorb most of the swing unless the government caps it. The government says data centres must pay for the grid they cause, but no published figures yet show they do.

Energy in, compute out

The fuel is imported and burned in Malaysia, the emissions and water use stay in Malaysia, and the computing largely goes to foreign-owned clouds, American and Chinese. Malaysia is in effect exporting its electricity as compute. Whether that is a good deal depends on what it keeps: tariffs that cover the gas, local jobs, and a say over who uses the chips.

8 · Leverage and weak points

Promises run far ahead of use, and water runs out first

Sources: RHB Research after meeting TNB, via Business Today, 11 Jul 2026; energy ministry via Malay Mail, 4 Nov 2026; water regulator SPAN via Free Malaysia Today, 10 Feb 2025.

Weak points

  • Most Peninsular power comes from imported coal, and the coal exit runs through new gas plants that will not arrive before 2028.
  • In 2024 the water regulator approved only 18% of the water data centres asked for.
  • A typical data centre needs only 30 to 50 permanent staff, up to about 200 for the largest (US benchmark cited by ISEAS).
  • Chip enforcement is rising: a server fraud case in Singapore, a 72-server customs seizure at KLIA, and two US prosecutions involving shipments via Malaysia.

Leverage

  • Both stacks need scarce power, water and land that Malaysia controls.
  • Ramp-or-pay rules and ten-year load plans now push bubble risk back onto developers.
  • Approvals now depend on surplus power and water, with water going to residents first (deputy minister, July 2026).
